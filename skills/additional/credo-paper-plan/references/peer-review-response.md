# Peer-Review Response

Use when reviews of a submitted paper arrive and the author wants a response or rebuttal planned. The goal is a response a reviewer can verify point by point, without promising what the author has not approved.

## Plan the response

- List every reviewer concern as its own item with the reviewer and location. No concern disappears from the plan, including the ones the author would rather not answer.
- Answer each item in its first sentence. Context and evidence follow the answer.
- Choose one disposition per item: a clarification of what the paper already says, existing evidence the reviewer missed (with its location), new evidence already completed, a revision the author commits to, or a limitation stated with its scope.
- Concede in bounded ways: correct an error, narrow a claim, qualify its conditions, or bound its scope. A concession names exactly what changes in the paper.
- Never present a plan as a result. An experiment not yet run is a plan, and "we will add" commits the author; write it only with the author's approval.
- Calibrate verbs to the evidence: "shows" and "demonstrates" for direct evidence, "supports" and "suggests" for partial evidence, "we hypothesize" for a mechanism, and "we have not evaluated" for what is untested.
- When one reviewer's score decides the outcome, spend the response budget on that reviewer's central objection first.

## Novelty disputes

When a reviewer says the work is not new, answer in three parts: the established ingredients, the exact new thing, and what the ingredients did not already imply. Cite the closest prior work by name.

## Deliver

Return the item list with dispositions and draft answers, the commitments that need the author's approval, and the concerns that remain open. Drafting the final prose uses the writing skill when available. Submitting the response stays with the author.
